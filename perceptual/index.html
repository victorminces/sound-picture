Sound Dimensions

A picture of how a sound is heard, not of how it was recorded. One ribbon, five properties. No targets and no score — the therapist reads it and guides from it.

Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. Everything is measured in the browser and thrown away as it goes.

Reading the ribbon

Height — sharpness
How high the sound sits, in the sense a listener means by it: a squeaky hiss against a deep one. This is the dimension that separates /s/ from a palatal or /sh/-like production, and it gets the vertical axis because position is the channel a child reads most easily. Measured as a weighted centre of the specific loudness pattern, which is near enough level-independent — a quiet /s/ and a loud one sit at the same height.
Thickness — loudness
Relative to this child’s own recent speech, never to an absolute standard. A web microphone reports no calibrated level, so an absolute number here would be invented.
Softness — spread
A focused hiss has a crisp edge; one smeared across the range feathers out. Measured in Bark, the unit in which the ear’s resolution is roughly constant, so a sound reads as wide because it is wide and not merely because it sits high.
Grain — roughness
Fast speckle, around 70 modulations a second: too quick to hear as separate events, so it fuses into a buzzy, gurgly, wet quality. This is the strongest candidate for the slushiness of a lateral release. It is measured as a peak at that rate against the modulation at neighbouring rates, not as an absolute depth, because noise modulates itself and how much depends on how broad it is.
Ripple — fluctuation
A slow waver in the edge, around 4 times a second: a gesture that will not hold still. A different percept from grain and an order of magnitude slower, which is why they are measured separately and shown separately.
Core — voicing
A bright filament down the middle of the ribbon, brighter the more clearly the larynx is running. /s/ has none; /z/ has a strong one.

These are perceptual dimensions — properties of how a sound is heard. They are deliberately not claims about where the tongue is. Inferring articulation from a microphone alone is a guess, and a display that makes it confidently would be lying. What is shown here is what a listener is responding to, which is also what every clinical rating and every labelled speech corpus is built on.

Nothing here has been tried with a child, and the roughness constants are calibrated against synthetic modulated noise rather than a real lateral production. Treat the numbers as comparable within a session and not across children.
